D-Block Elements: Mastering Transition Metals & Coordination Chemistry

Complete NEET dropper guide to cracking one of the highest-weightage inorganic chapters

Published: November 27, 2026 | Chemistry | Dropper Strategy

D-block elements (transition metals) account for 8-12% of NEET Chemistry questions, with coordination chemistry being the most heavily tested subtopic. As a dropper, you need a systematic approach to this chapter because it combines memorization, conceptual understanding, and application-based reasoning.

Many droppers struggle with d-block because they treat it as pure memory work. The reality: NEET tests your ability to predict electron configurations, understand oxidation states, recognize complex geometries, and apply crystal field theory. This article provides the exact framework successful droppers use to score 15+ marks from this single chapter.

Understanding D-Block Chemistry: Why This Chapter Matters for Droppers

The d-block consists of elements from Group 3 to Group 12 (scandium to zinc). What makes this chapter crucial for NEET droppers:

  • High repetition: NEET repeats questions on electronic configuration, ionization enthalpy, and oxidation states almost every year
  • Coordination chemistry overlap: About 60% of d-block questions involve complex formation, ligand types, and VSEPR theory
  • Straightforward MCQs: Unlike organic chemistry, most questions have definitive answers if you understand the core principles
  • Concept-based scoring: Few questions require memorization beyond basic properties—most test conceptual application

Droppers who score 18+ marks from this chapter follow a three-phase strategy: (1) master electron configuration rules, (2) understand oxidation state trends, (3) dominate coordination chemistry through visual learning. This chapter often becomes the difference between 500-550 and 550-600+ NEET scores.

Electronic Configuration and Oxidation States: The Foundation Droppers Miss

NEET asks 2-3 direct questions from electron configuration and oxidation states every year. Droppers fail here because they memorize without understanding the "why."

D-Block Electron Configuration Pattern

The standard pattern is (n-1)dxnsy. However, NEET focuses on three exceptions that appear repeatedly:

  • Chromium (Cr): [Ar] 3d5 4s1 (not 3d4 4s2) - half-filled d orbital stability
  • Copper (Cu): [Ar] 3d10 4s1 (not 3d9 4s2) - completely filled d orbital stability
  • Molybdenum (Mo): [Kr] 4d5 5s1 - same half-filled principle

Droppers memorize these but don't connect them to the examination pattern. NEET always follows up with: "Which has highest ionization enthalpy?" (Cr) or "Which has maximum number of unpaired electrons?" (Cr). Learn the exceptions with their consequences.

Dropper Strategy: Create a visual chart of Cr and Cu showing their actual configurations versus expected configurations. NEET has asked "Why does Cu+ form?" at least 4 times in the last decade. The answer: Cu naturally loses that extra 4s electron to achieve stable 3d10. This one concept answers 2-3 related questions.

Variable Oxidation States: The Pattern Droppers Exploit

Transition metals show variable oxidation states because (n-1)d and ns electrons have similar energies. NEET tests this through:

  • Identifying the most stable oxidation state (usually +2 for most, +3 for Fe, Cr)
  • Predicting which metal forms a particular complex with given oxidation state
  • Understanding why Mn7+ exists but Cu3+ doesn't

Critical pattern: Maximum oxidation state = Group number (Mn is Group 7, maximum oxidation state +7 in MnO4−). Minimum stable oxidation state = +2 for most. Droppers who remember this single rule can eliminate 40% of wrong options immediately.

Coordination Chemistry: Where 60% of D-Block Marks Come From

NEET's coordination chemistry questions follow predictable patterns that change yearly in substance but repeat in structure. As a dropper, recognizing these patterns is your highest-ROI study strategy.

Ligand Classification and Coordination Number Prediction

NEET tests three things repeatedly:

Concept High-Frequency Questions Dropper Must-Know
Ligand Types Identify monodentate vs polydentate; name chelating agents EDTA is tetridentate/hexadentate depending on how many metal coordination sites. NH3 is always monodentate. This distinction appears in 90% of multi-step questions.
Coordination Number Determine CN given ligand size and metal CN=4 for Pt2+ and Zn2+ (square planar and tetrahedral). CN=6 for Fe2+, Fe3+, Cr3+ (octahedral). Droppers who memorize these 5 combinations answer 70% of CN questions correctly.
Complex Stability Compare stability of two complexes Chelating effect: polydentate ligands form more stable complexes. Ionic charge: higher charge metals form stronger complexes. These two principles alone predict the answer 80% of the time.

Crystal Field Theory (CFT) and Geometry: The Visual Framework

Droppers fail CFT questions because they memorize d-orbital splitting diagrams without connecting them to geometry and magnetism. NEET always asks in combination: "In octahedral complex XY6, if the complex is paramagnetic, what is the d-orbital configuration?" OR "Square planar geometry indicates which dn configuration?"

Critical CFT facts NEET repeats:

  • Octahedral: t2g6eg0 (d6 like Fe2+ in [Fe(CN)6]4−) = diamagnetic (low spin). t2g4eg2 (d6 like Fe3+ in [Fe(H2O)6]3+) = paramagnetic (high spin)
  • Tetrahedral: always high spin due to smaller splitting energy
  • Square planar: always d8 (like Pt2+), always diamagnetic

Droppers who create a simple 3×3 grid (geometry × dn × magnetic property) can visualize and memorize these combinations much faster than trying to memorize abstract splitting diagrams.

Dropper Exam Strategy: NEET always includes one CFT question that asks for magnetic moment. Know this formula: μ = √[n(n+2)] where n = number of unpaired electrons. For octahedral high-spin d5 (5 unpaired): μ = √[5(7)] = √35 ≈ 5.92 BM. This single formula, combined with d-orbital filling, answers virtually every magnetic moment question correctly.

Naming and Isomerism: Quick Elimination Strategy

NEET includes 1-2 questions on complex nomenclature and isomerism. Droppers often skip these thinking they're too detailed, but systematic naming eliminates ambiguity:

  • Naming order: (1) Count and name ligands in alphabetical order (di-, tri-, tetra-) using modified names (NH3 = ammine, H2O = aqua), (2) Write metal name and oxidation state, (3) Add -ate if anionic
  • Isomerism: Geometric (cis/trans) for square planar and octahedral; linkage isomerism for ambidentate ligands like SCN−; ionization isomerism for different anions outside coordination sphere

Most NEET questions on isomerism show two formulas with same atoms but different arrangement, then ask "These are?" Droppers who understand why [Pt(NH3)2Cl2] exists in cis and trans forms (but not all geometries) can answer 90% of these questions without excessive memorization.

High-Yield Topics: What Droppers Must Prioritize in the Last 90 Days

If you're a dropper with limited time, focus on these highest-probability question types:

  • Color of coordination complexes: This appears in 70% of NEET papers. The color results from d-d transitions (absorption of specific wavelengths). Know: Cu2+ complexes = blue, Cr(III) = green, MnO4− = purple, Fe(III) = yellow-brown. NEET often shows a colored solution and asks for the metal or geometry
  • Stability series: Metal-ligand bond strength follows: CN− > NH3 > H2O > OH− > Cl− (spectrochemical series). Questions ask which complex is most/least stable; this series answers 100% of them
  • Spin-pairing energy vs crystal field splitting: This determines whether a complex is high-spin or low-spin. If CFSE > pairing energy, complex goes low-spin. This one concept eliminates the need to memorize individual complex magnetic properties
  • Coordination compounds in biological systems: Hemoglobin (Fe2+-O2 complex), Chlorophyll (Mg2+-porphyrin). NEET asks 1 question here almost every year
